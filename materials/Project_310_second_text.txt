INTRODUCTION1.1. Project StatementEastern International University occupies a large, multi-block campus in Binh Duong Province, Vietnam. The campus contains numerous academic buildings (Blocks B3, B8, B9, B10, B11), support facilities such as the Library, Canteen, and multiple Parking Zones, all connected by an internal network of pedestrian pathways and roads. New students, prospective applicants, visiting faculty, and delivery personnel frequently encounter difficulty locating specific facilities within this environment.The problem is compounded by the fact that commercial mapping services such as Google Maps, Apple Maps, and HERE Maps do not capture the fine-grained internal road topology of private campuses. While these platforms accurately represent the surrounding urban street network, they do not possess data about the pedestrian walkways, named internal roads (e.g., "side-b10-gate4", "circle", "road-canteen"), or the precise spatial relationships between academic blocks within the EIU boundary. A user relying on Google Maps alone would receive no navigational guidance once they enter the campus gate.This project addresses that gap by building a dedicated, lightweight mobile application â€” MyCampusApp â€” that provides indoor-grade navigation for the EIU campus using a custom GIS dataset. The application renders the campus map on top of a standard satellite/street base layer, overlays custom polygon and polyline layers derived from the university's own Shapefile data, and computes optimal walking routes between any two named landmarks using a graph-based pathfinding algorithm.1.2. Background and MotivationThe proliferation of smartphones has made mobile mapping an essential daily tool for billions of users worldwide. Navigation applications have advanced from simple turn-by-turn street guidance to rich, context-aware platforms integrating indoor positioning, augmented reality, and real-time traffic data. Institutions such as universities, hospitals, airports, and shopping centres have increasingly recognised that commercial maps do not meet their internal wayfinding needs, motivating the development of purpose-built campus navigation solutions.Several technical trends converge to make this project feasible without specialist hardware:Cross-platform mobile development frameworks such as React Native allow a single TypeScript codebase to compile to both iOS and Android, significantly reducing engineering effort compared to native development in Swift/Objective-C and Kotlin/Java.GIS-grade spatial data formats, particularly GeoJSON, provide a lightweight, human-readable encoding of geographic geometries (points, polylines, polygons) that can be bundled directly into a mobile application as a static asset â€” eliminating the need for a backend map tile server.Graph-based pathfinding algorithms, specifically A\* (A-star), are well-established in computer science and offer optimal shortest-path solutions for the scale of a campus road network (typically fewer than 1,000 nodes), allowing computation to occur entirely on-device in under one millisecond.The motivation for this specific project arose from the direct experience of students and visitors navigating the EIU campus. The campus's architecture â€” with parallel rows of similarly styled buildings â€” creates spatial disorientation, and the absence of visible directional signage compounds the problem. A digital navigation tool accessible on a personal smartphone represents the most scalable and low-maintenance solution.1.3. Objectives and ScopeObjectives:1. RELATED WORKS2.1. Campus Navigation SystemsCampus navigation systems have been an active area of research and commercial development for over a decade. Several approaches exist in the literature and in production:Web-Based GIS Portals: Many universities publish static online maps built with ArcGIS Online, Google Maps Platform, or Leaflet.js. These portals present a browser-accessible map with clickable building markers but offer no turn-by-turn routing for pedestrians. Their primary limitation is the requirement for an internet browser and no mobile-native experience.Dedicated Native Applications: Institutions such as MIT, Stanford, and the National University of Singapore have deployed iOS/Android applications that use the Google Maps SDK or Apple MapKit with custom overlays for internal navigation. These applications typically maintain a backend database of room-level points of interest and integrate with the institution's identity management system. Their limitation is the cost and complexity of maintaining two separate native codebases.Hybrid Applications (React Native / Flutter): More recent campus navigation projects have adopted cross-platform frameworks. Hybrid Applications (React Native / Flutter): More recent campus navigation projects have adopted cross-platform frameworks. In these systems, bundling a pre-computed routing graph as a local JSON asset, rather than querying a remote routing server over the network, is a best practice that significantly reduces routing latency and enables offline navigation.Indoor Navigation Research: For multi-storey buildings, GPS signals are unavailable, and researchers have explored Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacon triangulation, Wi-Fi fingerprinting (Mirowski et al., 2013), and visual positioning systems (VPS) using computer vision. These approaches are beyond the scope of this project but represent important directions for future extension.The approach taken by MyCampusApp most closely aligns with the static-asset hybrid model: all routing data is pre-processed and bundled with the application, ensuring that navigation functions correctly even without cellular network connectivity â€” a significant advantage in areas of poor coverage.2.2. Cross-Platform Mobile FrameworksThe selection of a development framework is a foundational architectural decision that affects developer productivity, application performance, UI fidelity, and long-term maintainability. Three frameworks dominated consideration for this project:Flutter (Dart, Google, 2018): Flutter compiles to native ARM code and uses its own rendering engine (Skia/Impeller) rather than platform-native UI widgets, producing highly consistent cross-platform appearance. Its map integration ecosystem (flutter_map, google_maps_flutter) is mature. However, Flutter's adoption of Dart â€” a language with limited prior exposure in academic curricula focused on JavaScript/TypeScript â€” was identified as a learning overhead risk for a time-constrained project.React Native (JavaScript/TypeScript, Meta, 2015): React Native renders native platform UI components through a JavaScript bridge (or the newer JSI architecture), giving applications a genuinely native look and feel. Its ecosystem is extensive: the `react-native-maps` library provides a production-grade wrapper around both Google Maps SDK (Android) and Apple MapKit (iOS). Critically, the development team possessed prior experience in React, making the framework the most productive choice.Ionic (Angular/React/Vue, HTML/CSS): Ionic wraps a web view inside a native shell, meaning the map rendering is performed by the browser engine rather than the native map SDK. This imposes significant performance penalties on map tile rendering and gesture handling, making it unsuitable for a map-centric application.Decision: React Native with the Expo managed workflow was selected for the following reasons:- TypeScript-first development with strong type safety for geographic coordinate types.- `react-native-maps` provides direct access to Google Maps SDK with Polygon, Polyline, and Marker primitives needed for the spatial overlay system.- Expo's managed workflow simplifies SDK integration (location, linear gradient, router) without requiring ejection to bare React Native.- `expo-location` provides a unified, permission-managed GPS API across iOS and Android.Table 2.1. Graph ConstructionThe graph construction module reads the campus road dataset and builds an in-memory, bidirectional weighted graph using an adjacency list structure. This process occurs once at application startup and is shared across all subsequent route queries. The construction process is as follows:1. Feature Iteration: Each GeoJSON Feature whose geometry type is `MultiLineString` is processed. Features with `road` ending in `-in` are skipped.2. Coordinate Parsing: For each LineString within the MultiLineString, consecutive coordinate pairs are processed as directed edges. GeoJSON encodes coordinates as `[longitude, latitude]` (note: reversed from the conventional latitude-first order); this is corrected during parsing.3. Node Key Generation: Each geographic coordinate is converted to a canonical string key by rounding to 5 decimal places (approximately 1-metre precision): `nodeKey(lat, lng) = "${lat.toFixed(5)},${lng.toFixed(5)}"`. This allows nearby-but-identical coordinates from different features to be correctly identified as the same graph node.4. Edge Weight: The edge weight between two adjacent nodes is the Haversine great-circle distance in metres, computed by `haversine(lat1, lng1, lat2, lng2)`.5. Snap-to-GraphBecause building entrance coordinates and GPS positions rarely fall exactly on a road network node, a nearest-neighbour search is performed before every route computation. This process scans all available nodes and identifies the one closest to the given coordinate using the Haversine formula. Given the relatively small size of the campus road network, this linear scan is fast enough to be performed in real time without any noticeable delay.3.3.3. A\* PathfindingThe routing engine implements the classic A* best-first search algorithm. Internally, the algorithm maintains three key data structures: a cost map that tracks the actual distance travelled from the start to each visited node (g-score), an estimated total cost map combining the actual distance with the heuristic distance to the goal (f-score), and a predecessor map used to reconstruct the final path once the destination is reached. A priority queue ordered by f-score ensures that the most promising node is always explored first.Heuristic h(n): The Haversine distance from node n to the goal â€” an admissible heuristic since the straight-line distance never overestimates the true walking distance on a road network. This guarantees that A\* finds the globally optimal (shortest) path.Algorithm Steps:1. Initialise: push start node with f = h(start, goal); g(start) = 0.2. Loop: pop the node with smallest fScore.3. If current == goal: reconstruct path by following `cameFrom` pointers and return the ordered `LatLng[]` array.4. For each neighbour: compute tentative g score. If better than previously recorded, update g/f scores, record predecessor, add to open set.5. If open set empties without reaching goal: return `null` (no path exists). End-to-End Data FlowWhen a user initiates a route, the following sequence takes place:Â 1.Â Origin resolution:Â The starting point is determined either from the device's live GPS position or from a building the user has manually selected.Â 2.Â Snap to graph:Â Both the origin and destination coordinates are mapped to their nearest nodes on the campus road network, ensuring the route begins and ends on a valid road segment.Â 3.Â Route computation:Â The routing engine calculates the shortest path between the two snapped nodes and returns an ordered list of geographic waypoints.Â 4.Â Map rendering:Â The computed waypoints are drawn on the map as a visible route path.Â 5.Â Auto-zoom:Â The map camera automatically adjusts its zoom level and position to frame the entire route within the screen, accounting for the bottom control panel.3.4. User Interface (UI) Design3.4.1. Design PrinciplesThe UI design of MyCampusApp follows a dark-mode navigation aesthetic, inspired by the UI patterns of Google Maps Night Mode, Apple Maps, and Waze. The design priorities were:- Map visibility: The map must dominate the screen. Overlaid UI elements are minimal, semi-transparent where possible, and positioned at screen edges.- Single-thumb reachability: The route planner controls are placed at the bottom of the screen, within comfortable reach of a user's thumb when holding a phone in one hand.- Status clarity: The current route state (no route / route active) must be immediately legible at a glance.3.4.2. Main Map ScreenThe main screen is composed of the following layers:- MapView (Full-screen): Google Maps with `mapType="standard"`, overlaid with:- A semi-transparent blue-grey Polygon tracing the campus boundary.- Custom Marker badges for each of the 12 landmarks (white pill with blue border; active destination highlighted in solid blue).- The dual-layer route Polyline (white stroke, width 9, beneath the blue stroke, width 5) for a Google Maps navigation visual style.- A "You are here" dot (pulsing blue circle) at the user's GPS position.- Vignette gradients: Four `<LinearGradient>` overlays (top, bottom, left, right edges) provide a subtle darkening of the map perimeter, focusing the user's visual attention on the campus centre.3.4.3. Route Panel (Bottom Sheet)The route planner is implemented as a draggable bottom sheet panel that can be pulled up or pushed down by the user. It snaps smoothly between two fixed heights â€” an expanded state showing the full route controls, and a collapsed state showing only the panel title. The transition between states uses a spring animation, and the panel content fades in and out accordingly as the height changes.3.4.4. Building Picker ModalThe building picker is presented as a full-screen modal that slides up from the bottom of the screen. It contains a real-time search bar that instantly filters the list of campus landmarks as the user types. A 'My Location' option is available at the top of the list when selecting the starting point, allowing users to navigate from their current GPS position. Route Planning â€” Marker Tap FlowAs an alternative to the picker modal, the user can tap any building marker badge directly on the map. Observations confirmed that the A* algorithm produced reasonable and visually accurate results for all tested cases.4.3.2. PerformanceRoute computation was noticeably fast across all tested cases, with no visible delay from the user's perspective. Zoom-Dependent Marker Clustering: At low zoom levels, overlapping building markers should be clustered into a single aggregate marker showing the count. Indoor Floor-Level Navigation: The current system navigates between buildings but not within them.
